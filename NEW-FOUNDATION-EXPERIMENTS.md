# New Foundation Experiments
**Status: exploratory only — nothing here is canon or approved.**

## Why this round is different
Earlier brainstorming leaned on familiar mystery objects, hidden places, erased histories, and mythic explanations. This round tests world-generating mechanisms: rules that would shape daily life, politics, crime, family, warfare, comedy, exploration, and mystery at once. Each mechanism must produce many different kinds of stories without explaining every event through one central secret.

Originality cannot be guaranteed by a premise sentence alone. These are starting mechanisms to stress-test for distinctiveness, internal logic, visual storytelling, character consequences, and long-range story potential. They must be compared against existing fiction before any is treated as sufficiently original.

## Experiment 1 — Consequences Without Their Causes
Sometimes a real consequence appears before the action that causes it. This is not ordinary time travel, prophecy, or a vision: the consequence is physically present, while its cause has not yet occurred.

Potential consequences:
- Courts debate how to judge crimes whose evidence exists before the act.
- States exploit consequences to plan wars; opponents try to make the expected cause fail.
- Families, merchants, and ordinary workers adapt to debts, inheritances, injuries, and celebrations that arrive early.
- A small personal contradiction could expose a larger political scheme.

Central pressure-test: Is the cause inevitable, or can people create a contradiction between an existing consequence and its missing cause?

Risks: It may feel like a familiar time-manipulation story; without precise limits it can dissolve suspense. Define what can arrive early, what cannot, and what happens when the cause never occurs.

## Experiment 2 — No One Can Own the Same Thing Twice
A proposed natural law: after something has genuinely belonged to a person, it can never belong to that same person again. The scope is deliberately undecided: it might apply to objects, land, titles, inventions, names, discoveries, or authority—but not necessarily all of them.

Potential consequences:
- Inheritance, commerce, law, and government evolve around passing things onward.
- Heists become puzzles of transfer and custody rather than simple theft.
- A ruler who once relinquished a country may be unable to rule it again.
- Inventors, families, and communities develop competing ways to share or regain access without ownership.

Central pressure-test: What makes something truly yours: possession, legal recognition, use, sacrifice, or something else?

Risks: If applied to everything without boundaries, it becomes an arbitrary puzzle and makes everyday life implausible. Choose a narrow, legible rule and explore its social consequences.

## Experiment 3 — Civilizations With Incompatible Standards of Truth
Different societies have discovered that reality responds, under specific conditions, to different ways of validating claims. One may require public witnesses, another repeatable tests, another promises kept across generations, and another claims that survive formal challenge.

These are not merely opinions or religions: each method has real but bounded effects, and each culture has developed institutions around it.

Potential consequences:
- Treaties fail because the parties disagree about what makes a promise binding.
- A military victory can remain unresolved under two incompatible standards.
- Diplomats, fraudsters, judges, scientists, and families exploit or bridge the differences.
- A marriage, discovery, election, or border dispute can become an adventure rather than a lecture.

Central pressure-test: What happens when two societies are both correct according to different rules that make their worlds function?

Risks: Could become abstract or didactic. Rules must be observable, limited, and dramatized through concrete stakes; no culture should be reduced to one gimmick.

## Experiment 4 — A World That Produces Things Before Their Uses Are Known
The world continually produces real but seemingly useless things: materials, organisms, structures, devices, and natural phenomena. They do not all come from one ancient maker or serve one hidden plan. Some have ordinary ecological or physical explanations; some remain dangerous or inexplicable.

Potential consequences:
- A dismissed organism later becomes vital to a damaged ecosystem.
- A material considered worthless changes the outcome of an expedition or conflict.
- An apparently useless structure becomes a town, then a political flashpoint.
- Collectors, smugglers, scientists, artists, opportunists, and explorers disagree about what deserves preservation.

Central pressure-test: Can people recognize the importance of something before they know what it is for?

Risks: It could become a random catalogue of quirky curiosities. Each phenomenon needs coherent properties, consequences, and limits; many should remain unrelated to the main saga.

## Evaluation protocol
For each experiment, ask:
1. Does it immediately generate a memorable scene, not just an intriguing explanation?
2. Can it sustain comedy, intimacy, politics, conflict, exploration, danger, and wonder without forcing every story into the same pattern?
3. Can the world feel inhabited and economically/socially plausible?
4. Does it produce meaningful choices and consequences for characters?
5. Is the mechanism distinguishable from familiar genre conventions after comparison with existing stories?
6. Can the larger saga emerge from consequences without every local mystery secretly being part of one master plan?
7. Can it be combined with other mechanisms without turning into an overstuffed premise?

## Current conclusion
None of these is selected. They are not claims of absolute novelty, and none should be promoted to canon just because its wording sounds unusual. The next step is to find stronger, stranger collisions between mechanisms—or discard all four if they still feel familiar.
