# Master Blueprint

**Status:** Foundation document — creative direction approved; story premise still open.

## 1. The ambition

Create an original, cinematic adventure-fantasy franchise told through sequential movies. It should be memorable for discovery, mystery, world-building, characters, emotional payoff, and long-term continuity.

Audiences should wonder what lies beyond the next horizon, debate clues between films, care about the characters, and see earlier movies differently as new truths emerge. The goal is not merely to make audiences finish the films, but to make them want to remain in this universe and eagerly anticipate what happens next.

## 2. Genre, scope and tone

The foundation combines:
- **Adventure:** expeditions, dangerous journeys, rival seekers, puzzles, escapes, discoveries, heists, and consequential choices.
- **Mystery:** legends, unanswered questions, misleading evidence, revelations, and carefully planted clues.
- **Fantasy:** extraordinary places, distinct powers, unfamiliar beings, fantastic creatures, dangerous territories, and a coherent sense of wonder.
- **Human drama:** friendship, humour, rivalry, love, grief, betrayal, loyalty, sacrifice, pain, reconciliation, and lasting consequences.
- **Other genres as the story needs them:** action, horror, political intrigue, war, crime, suspense, comedy, and intimate personal stories.

The tone can shift naturally across the saga: funny, playful, wondrous, suspenseful, frightening, emotionally intimate, dark when appropriate, and epic when earned. Not every scene needs to do everything.

## 3. The living universe

The world must be more than civilizations and political systems. It should feel like a complete fictional reality with room for things the audience cannot yet imagine.

- **Distinct lands and environments:** territories should have their own identity, beauty, dangers, ecology, histories, and story possibilities—not merely different scenery.
- **Many kinds of people and beings:** humans and other peoples or species may have different abilities, bodies, customs, needs, and ways of understanding the world.
- **Distinct powers:** abilities should create varied possibilities for combat, travel, survival, investigation, deception, rescue, craft, and everyday life. Avoid making raw strength the only meaningful measure of power.
- **Creatures with independent lives:** dragons, dinosaur-like giants, colossal serpents, predators, intelligent beings, guardians, and wholly original species can inhabit the world. They need not all be intelligent, tameable, friendly, or secretly misunderstood.
- **Creature relationships and motives:** some creatures may help travellers, form bonds, hunt people, compete with one another, defend territory, protect their young, or guard something they believe must never fall into the wrong hands. Others may be dangerous simply because of their nature.
- **Secrets with consequences:** guarded places, species, knowledge, objects, and phenomena should raise meaningful questions about who has the right to discover, use, protect, or destroy them.
- **Life beyond the main cast:** creatures, societies, economies, ecosystems, traditions, conflicts, and events must continue to exist when the protagonist is absent.

These are creative-direction commitments, not a settled list of canon species, powers, places, or rules. Specific inventions must be original, purposeful, and developed only when useful to the story.

## 4. Franchise structure

- The films are sequential and connected.
- Each film needs a distinct identity and satisfying central adventure.
- Each film resolves meaningful parts of its own conflict while advancing the larger saga.
- New films deepen the story rather than merely postpone answers.
- Different films may lean into different genres: for example, a heist, expedition, mystery, survival horror, political crisis, war, or creature-centred adventure.
- Humour, emotion, action, horror, fantasy, lore, and spectacle should arise naturally from the story rather than feel like disconnected ingredients.
- The number of films is undecided; let the story determine it.
- Plan major truths, character turning points, and the ending early enough for intentional foreshadowing, while leaving room to improve the journey.

## 5. The audience experience

Aim for a cycle of:
1. **Immediate invitation:** an intriguing situation and an adventure worth entering before the mythology is explained.
2. **Attachment:** characters, relationships, humour, and ordinary moments that make the audience care.
3. **Escalation:** complications, dangers, discoveries, rivalries, and choices that change the situation.
4. **Surprise:** evidence or events that challenge the audience's current understanding without feeling arbitrary.
5. **Payoff:** a satisfying resolution to the film's main conflict, with real consequences.
6. **Renewed curiosity:** a larger question or possibility that makes the next film desirable without making the current film feel incomplete.

The goal is **continuity without predictability**. Earlier details should gain meaning in hindsight, but the world must not reduce every event, creature, power, and relationship to one explanatory gimmick.

## 6. Originality

Use existing adventure, fantasy, mystery, and serial storytelling as craft inspiration only. Do not copy another franchise's premise, characters, iconic objects, mythology, scenes, or plot structure. Our world, central hook, rules, mysteries, creatures, powers, and emotional core must have their own identity.

A premise must be exciting before its lore is explained. It must generate vivid scenes, human choices, and multiple distinct kinds of stories—not merely sound clever in a summary.

## 7. Development order

1. Discover the central franchise premise.
2. Define the world and what makes exploration compelling.
3. Identify the core mystery and the truth behind it.
4. Develop the world's powers, creatures, lands, and other extraordinary possibilities in service of story.
5. Sketch the saga-wide arc and individual movie arcs.
6. Develop the protagonist and ensemble to fit the story.
7. Build continuity, clue, and payoff tracking.
8. Outline each film, then write screenplays.
9. Develop visual concepts only when requested.

## 8. Protagonist

**Status: OPEN QUESTION / intentionally deferred.**

The hero will be created from scratch after the core premise is stronger. Name, age, background, detailed personality, motivation, abilities, relationships, and long-term arc are undecided. Earlier character notions are not canon.

## 9. Creative quality tests

- Is the idea distinctive, or does it feel borrowed?
- Does it immediately ignite imagination and curiosity?
- Does it create compelling scenes and choices, not just lore?
- Does the world feel alive beyond the protagonist and beyond human societies?
- Do powers and creatures enable varied stories instead of just functioning as combat tools?
- Can it sustain different kinds of films without repetition?
- Does the mystery have a real answer?
- Are clues fair in hindsight without making the story obvious?
- Do discoveries have consequences?
- Does each film deliver a satisfying experience on its own?
- Will the ending pay off the emotional and story promises made earlier?

## 10. Canon discipline

Only mark something CANON after explicit agreement. Keep proposals separate from decisions. When canon changes, record what changed, why, and which documents need updating.
